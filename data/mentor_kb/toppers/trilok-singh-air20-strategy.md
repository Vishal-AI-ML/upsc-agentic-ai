# Trilok Singh - Strategy Note (AIR 20)

## Profile & journey (why this story matters)
- Defence background; original dream was Air Force pilot. Got NDA recommendation (after several attempts) but was declared permanently medically unfit (bone anatomy / asymmetrical chest). Scored ~96 percentile in JEE Mains as a backup.
- Shifted to Civil Services partly guided by an elder sibling who had appeared for CSE Mains (NET-JRF in Political Science). That in-house guidance shaped early resource choices.
- Education: BSc Maths (Hons), Kirori Mal College, DU. Initially wanted Maths optional; dropped it because COVID forced online classes and a conceptual subject like Maths needs regular in-person coaching. Switched to PSIR.
- Attempt arc:
  - First CSE Prelims attempt: failed by a big margin (~21 marks short; scored ~71, cutoff ~87-88). Given while juggling final-year college exams (Prelims just days after the last college paper).
  - Next attempt: cleared Prelims comfortably (strong CSAT, ~100), but Mains score was ~674, roughly 68 marks below cutoff.
  - Following attempt: cleared Prelims again and Mains jumped to ~851 (a ~178 mark increase); interview ~165; final selection in the top ranks.
- Also cleared/attempted parallel exams: RAS (Rajasthan state PCS) Mains, IB ACIO (tiers + interview). Preparing for these kept him "always in study/writing mode," which he credits for factual sharpness.
- Core lesson from the whole arc: **be brutally honest with yourself.** Almost everyone tells themselves they missed the cutoff by 2 marks or one question; real progress starts when you accept the true gap and diagnose it precisely.

## Mindset & self-analysis (his biggest strength)
- Habit since NDA days: identify mistakes very precisely after every attempt, then fix exactly those - do not panic-change everything.
- Contrast: some peers who failed Prelims changed every source for every subject. He instead pinpointed "my weak areas are Science & Tech and Environment" and worked only on those.
- Dissolve ego fast. However big a past achievement (school topper, NDA rank), mistakes still happen - name them and move on.
- Do not over-correct. Keep a balance; keep what already works.
- Treat advice like a diagnosis, not a prescription. Universal advice doesn't exist; take inputs from many, then choose what suits your background, comfort, and finances.

## Prelims strategy
### How he analyses a Prelims paper
- Splits into ~7 core subjects: Polity, Economics, History, Science & Tech, Environment, Geography (+ current). For each: how many questions came, how many he attempted, how many went wrong. Takes 2-2.5 hours but gives a clear picture.
- Found clearly that in Science & Tech + Environment he was attempting ~17-20 but getting only ~5-6 right - a precise, actionable finding.
- Reviews "lucky guesses" too: in mocks, if you attempted 88 and 27 were 50-50 guesses, track the right/wrong ratio of guesses. A guess going right does NOT mean you know it - review those anyway. Pure static that you know (e.g., Article 32) can be skipped in review.

### Handling the new "weird/tough" Prelims trend
- Accept low marginal utility of ultra-tough/illogical questions (e.g., an obscure sports/world-cup fact). Don't chase 10 compilations for these; instead invest that time revising static.
- Prioritise integrated current+static questions - these are solvable if static is strong (his example: an "activated charcoal / adsorbent" question framed via current affairs but answerable from basic PCM knowledge).
- Where there's a clear indicator that questions come straight from NCERT (esp. physical geography), never lose those. "At least don't make that mistake."
- If your static base is strong (a legacy of NDA/CDS prep), you can attempt more confidently.

### Spaced revision system (his signature routine)
- Treat ~7 subjects. First revision cycle: ~7-8 days per subject -> ~55-60 days total for one full pass.
- Daily within that: ~5-6 hours on the main subject, ~2 hours on its PYQs, ~1-1.5 hours newspaper/current -> ~10-hour day.
- Give sectional tests to the toughest/most uncomfortable subjects (e.g., Ancient/Medieval history).
- Then shorten each cycle: 2nd pass ~5-6 days/subject, 3rd pass ~3-4 days/subject.
- **Reverse revision** in the last ~7 days: start from the most static subject you won't forget (Polity), then Physical Geography, Economics, History, then Science & Tech + Environment last (mix of facts + concepts), and one day for highlighted current-affairs magazine.

### Resources & tests
- PYQ workbooks followed diligently, including CDS / CAPF questions for extra practice.
- Science & Tech + Environment: found a single "one-stop" solution/source instead of the heavy, boring standard books he'd struggled with earlier - a turning point for those two subjects.
- Mocks: gave them properly the year he cleared (he had skipped mocks the year college exams clashed). Used free Sunday simulators too. In a later attempt he cut mocks to near-zero when time was tight and still cleared, because objective aptitude had matured - so mocks are a tool, not a mandate.
- Economic Survey: read personally for the first time - highlighted all chapters and made notes; hugely helped GS3 (and even GS1/current-linked topics).

## Mains strategy
### The big turnaround (674 -> 851)
- Year he scored 674: the fatal error was spending Prelims-to-Mains gap **making notes from scratch** (5 hrs GS notes + 5 hrs PSIR notes daily), so answer-writing practice suffered.
- Diagnosis: content was fine; execution/structure/practice were not. So next year:
  - **Denser content:** if writing ~155 words, make all 155 count - no padding.
  - **Sharper structure:** lift the question's own words into headings; decide sub-parts fast.
  - Concise self-made notes (whole GS in ~200 pages) so full GS could be revised in a day and answers replicated easily.
- His framing (echoing many toppers): content gets you to ~380 in GS; marks above that come from **structure**. A trained answer-writer decides within ~30 seconds (max 1 minute) of reading a question: sub-heading 1, sub-heading 2, whether a way-forward fits, what the intro and conclusion will be - because filling two pages itself needs ~6 minutes, leaving only ~1 minute to think.

### Notes philosophy (important)
- Big myth: judging notes by page count. Wrong lens. Open notes and ask: is the content good, does it solve PYQs, does it help me write answers? That's the mindset needed to clear this exam.
- Don't fear thick material. Summarise it yourself. He self-wrote condensed GS notes (e.g., ~48 pages for all of GS3 from a bulky module; and digital hand-written notes on iPad). **The act of summarising is the learning** - do it yourself rather than hunting for someone's ready short notes.
- Personal innovation example: fit all paramilitary/CAPF forces onto one page - a central India map, each force annotated (role, challenge, one common way-forward, border-specific notes for BSF/ITBP/coastal). Everyone has their own way of visualising; use your own mind.

### Peer-group answer writing
- A strong peer group was a major benefit. They fixed a topic daily (Democracy, Rights, India-US, etc.), each wrote ~5 questions, then evaluated each other's copies.
- Double benefit: morning revision + evening writing = reinforcement; and reading others' copies teaches new angles. Covered the whole optional syllabus this way in ~45 days.
- For GS he mostly relied on a limited number of full/half-length tests plus this daily writing - not an expensive test series.
- He never took a paid optional test series in any attempt.

### GS3 (a subject where 100+ is rare)
- Demarcate where to write MORE points vs where to EXPLAIN more. Internal Security and Agriculture -> maximise points/hits (list all challenges, global best practices; don't take these "low-hanging fruit" lightly - the syllabus is detailed and questions repeat with slight twists). Science & Tech and Disaster Management -> need 4-5 lines of real explanation (e.g., water purification technology).
- Add value: maps, diagrams, Venn diagrams (e.g., hazard-risk-vulnerability), integrate local + global best practices (e.g., for SDG targets), pointed intros and conclusions.
- Economic Survey is the single best source for quality data/graphs across Economics, Environment, even Society (e.g., ultra-processed food data linking to Society).

### Data-writing tweak
- Earlier he wrote long "According to NFHS-5..." lead-ins that bloated answers. Later he made data citations crisp (learned from topper copies) to save length.

### GS1 & Society
- World History became a strength via RAS prep (a separate 30-mark section there) - could sprinkle dates, thinkers, inventors, technologies into answers (e.g., causes of WWII with factual detail), which makes an answer stand out.
- Society: use organic, real-life examples (inter-caste/inter-religion; e.g., a state scheme giving money for inter-caste marriage), newspaper-derived examples (an ad campaign on communal harmony), and separate maps for disparity vs diversity.

### Essay & Ethics (his weaker/rebuilt areas)
- Essay caution from a bad first Mains: he once picked a philosophical/abstract topic, wrote 4-5 pages, then got blocked mid-way and panicked seeing others write. Lesson: choose the topic you can sustain for the full length; build a clean anecdotal intro + structured body + conclusion; don't gamble on abstract topics you can't carry.
- Ethics needed a genuinely new approach - he rebuilt conceptual understanding via structured lectures rather than rote notes, then practised.

### Paper-day discipline (hard-won)
- Do NOT get sucked into Telegram "was the paper hard?" chambers before/after papers. He wasted a crucial pre-Mains day discussing an Essay paper on Telegram instead of revising ~500 pages of notes for the next day's GS1/GS2 - "how stupid that was" in hindsight.
- Between papers, use a proper study space, not a relaxed flat with tea breaks and casual scrolling - that relaxed mode hurt an earlier attempt.
- On 20-mark questions where the pen "stalls after two pages," the fix is deeper understanding + revision, not panic-switching the question choice.

## Working aspirants / routine / discipline
- He often ran three tracks at once (UPSC + RAS + IB): mornings 6am-12pm on UPSC (static + PYQ), a fixed ~30-min afternoon nap (never sacrificed food/sleep quality - stayed fresh), evenings on state-PCS via a "5 questions daily, then check" active-recall channel (~450 questions over 90 days), ~1 hour Hindi at night for state PCS.
- On early rising: he treats the "5am club" edge as a psychological/mental construct - clocking ~5 hours by noon gives a genuine confidence boost. But it's person-to-person; if morning sleep hits, a short nap and restart is fine. Consistency of quality hours matters more than the clock.
- Job/security note: if working, keep the job at least till Prelims (Prelims has become unpredictable; quitting adds pressure if it doesn't clear). Use the Prelims-to-Mains gap for full immersion.

## Interview
- Board experience: a cordial board (he names a specific chairman known for a relaxed environment); pressure felt low.
- Two tricky moments: (1) asked to state the Army's organisation from section to top - he blanked on "division" and "corps" mid-way; lesson: know your DAF/background cold. (2) A deliberately provocative "let's remove the Army / it's just expense" line - they were testing institutional-values composure; staying calm (a habit from NDA SSB pressure) helped.
- Newspaper reading pays off most in the interview: an unexpected "parking fee" discussion was manageable only because he'd read a related Indian Express article - "even the board members are reading the same articles."
- His interview marks were relatively low (~165) but a very strong Mains carried the final rank. Message: don't treat interview in isolation; total matters.
- Debunk the myth: viral "UPSC riddles" on reels are fake; the interview is professional and largely linked to your DAF/background and current affairs.

## Mental health & the human cost (he stresses this hard)
- "Insider" result-leak culture wrecks mental health. He suggests UPSC should pre-announce a result date to calm nerves. Personal anecdote of the host underlined how a leaked "not-in-the-list" message the night before a result can be devastating and often wrong (fake PDFs circulate; AI-generated fake lists looked genuine).
- Constant comparison is the biggest drain: peers in NDA, corporate, or medical PG earning well; watching their social media. His fixes: keep Instagram uninstalled / browser-only monthly check; consciously do life things you're "missing" (went to watch an IPL match, a tennis final) so the journey doesn't cost you everything.
- Acknowledge sadness openly; normalise mental-health conversations (he speaks as a person who has seen how stigma stops people from admitting they're struggling). Lean on a supportive peer group and parents; he used Hindi poetry and mentor mental-health sessions to cope.
- Job pressure, money pressure, and family/social pressure (including "marriage-market" toxicity) are real - name them and manage them; keep physical activity (he played basketball, watched sport).
- Anchor: your happiness is not tied to UPSC. Prepare a strong Plan B / exit strategy before starting; decide how many attempts and how long, because these are your most productive years.

## Why he moved to service (the drive that developed later)
- Early motive was just "a good Group-A job." Over time, seeing ground realities (a PHC running child immunisation and COVID vaccination together without separation; garbage burned because collection charged ~Rs 50/month - he thought: auto-add it to the electricity bill so it's opt-out, not opt-in) built genuine administrative drive.

## Final message (for those who didn't clear Prelims)
- Beginners (1st attempt): stay in "Mains mode" - the three-month post-Prelims flood of resources is a gift; collect them, run a test series, do optional, as if you're giving Mains.
- Two+ attempts done: also look at alternatives/backups - CDS, IB ACIO, EPFO, state PCS (e.g., UPPCS), and the remuneration from test-series/teaching work.
- Universally: analyse mistakes fast, dissolve ego, don't over-correct, keep a good peer group, prioritise mental health, and shift environment (home vs city) based on your own diagnosis - not blind copying. Be mature enough to take only what's relevant to you.
- Above all: persevere. "You may only see darkness at one bend of the tunnel; light comes right after. Stay hopeful, keep working, and stay happy - never compromise your physical and mental health."
